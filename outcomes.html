Learning Outcomes

Ethics

Before this class I would look at if the AI works or not and that's it. But in week 3 I learned that an AI can work the way it was made and still end up hurting people. We went over three ways this can happen which are algorithmic bias, calibrated trust, and dual-use.

Algorithmic bias

In lecture we looked at a resume screener where two people had the same skills and the same experience, but the one who had a “Women’s” club on their resume got around 16 points lower every time. The tool never even knew what gender they were, it just picked it up from the club name.

That’s what a proxy variable is. It’s basically something that ends up standing in for a trait that the tool never asks about.

Then in lab we looked at Mobley v. Workday. The lawsuit says that Workday’s tool was using gaps in someone’s work history as a proxy. Older people and people with disabilities are more likely to have gaps, so they could get rejected even though the tool never asked about their age or disability. The case is still going on though so nothing has been proven yet.

Calibrated trust

Calibrated trust is about knowing when you should trust what an AI tells you and when you shouldn’t. Our rule was to ask yourself if you can check the answer yourself, how bad it would be if it’s wrong, and if you can undo it if it is wrong.

In lab tested Claude and ChatGPT with some medical questions. For the trouble breathing one, both of them told me to call 911 and use an EpiPen, which was way better than the fake app from lecture that just said “wait and see.” But the questions I asked were pretty easy and obvious. In the Winters case ChatGPT supposedly gave bad advice over a long conversation, so I don’t think you can fully trust it when it comes to serious stuff.

Dual-use

Dual-use is when a tool that was made for something good gets used for something bad. Voice cloning can help someone who lost their voice talk in their own voice again. But scammers are using it to fake calls from family members asking for money, and they only need like 3 seconds of someone’s voice to do it.

I think the company should take some of the blame too since this kind of thing is pretty easy to see coming. One way to fix it would be to make people read a random sentence out loud live before the tool lets them clone a voice.

History

Week 1 showed me that AI goes back much further than ChatGPT. The big pattern I noticed is that rule-based AI, like expert systems, kept breaking outside of what it was built for, so the field switched to learning from data instead. I wanted to see if that same pattern shows up somewhere we didn't talk about in class. I looked at the history of machine translation, which is getting a computer to translate one language into another. It started in January 1954 with the Georgetown-IBM experiment. They used an IBM 701 to automatically translate more than sixty Russian sentences into English. It worked off a small dictionary and handwritten grammar rules, which is the same "if this, then that" approach as early AI. The researchers said translation could be a solved problem in three to five years, and since it was the Cold War and the US wanted to read Russian documents, the government put a lot of money into it. It didn't work out. The system did fine on its small set of sentences but struggled once the dictionary got bigger, because real language is messy and the same word can mean different things depending on the sentence. In 1966, the government's ALPAC report found that ten years of research hadn't lived up to what was promised. Machine translation was slower, more expensive, and less accurate than just paying a human, so funding got cut way down. That's basically the same thing the Lighthill Report did to AI in 1973, except it happened to translation seven years earlier. The comeback followed the same pattern too. Instead of writing more rules, researchers switched to statistical methods that learned from huge amounts of text that was already translated by people. Google Translate used this kind of system for about ten years. Then in 2016 Google switched to a neural network, and in side-by-side tests it cut translation errors by about 60 percent. The next year the transformer came out, and it was actually built to make translation better. That's the same design ChatGPT is built on now. This connects to the McCarthy essay we read. In my response, I argued that when McCarthy answers people like Searle who say AI is impossible, he isn't really making a philosophy argument about minds, he's making an engineering one about how the system is built. The Georgetown team had that same engineer mindset. They didn't care if the computer actually "understood" Russian, they just wanted it to put out the right English. The problem is they thought that would be easy. Translating well takes a lot of common sense knowledge about the world, like knowing which meaning of a word fits the sentence, and that's the kind of knowledge McCarthy says an AI has to have built in. So their engineering approach made sense, they just way underestimated how much the system needed to know. What I took from this is that the timeline from Week 1 isn't just something that happened to AI in general. One specific problem went through every stage: the early hype, a winter when the rules couldn't handle the real world, a comeback from learning with data, and then deep learning. It also shows how much a field depends on hype and funding. The Georgetown team promised way too much way too fast, and when it didn't happen, the money disappeared for decades. With how much hype there is around AI right now, I think that's worth remembering.

Search

Weeks 4 and 5 were about how an AI finds a path from where it starts to a goal, and what changes when someone is fighting back. Everything in class used robots like Scout and games like chess, so I applied both kinds of search to cybersecurity instead. BloodHound is a tool that maps out a company's Active Directory, which is the system that controls who has access to what on a Windows network. It treats the network like a graph. Users, computers, and groups are the nodes, and things like "is a member of" or "is an admin on" are the edges. In search terms, the initial state is one user account an attacker got into, and the goal test is whether that account has Domain Admin access. The transition model is following an edge, like using an admin login on a computer to get to the next account, and the path cost is the number of hops. BloodHound's "shortest path to Domain Admins" uses breadth-first search, going from both ends at the same time. BFS makes sense here because every hop costs the same, so the first path BFS finds is the one with the fewest steps. The interesting part is that both attackers and defenders use it. Attackers use it to find the fastest way to take over a network. Defenders run it on their own network to find those paths first and cut them off by removing permissions people don't need. BloodHound assumes the network just sits there. In real life, the IT team fights back, so I made my own game for that. An attacker already got past the firewall and the defender is trying to stop them. They take turns. The defender patches one server, and the attacker takes over one unpatched server connected to something they already own. The game goes two rounds, and the score is the total value of what the attacker owns. The network has Web (worth 2) leading to DB (worth 5), and Mail (worth 1) leading to HR (worth 3). The attacker is MAX and wants a high score. The defender is MIN and wants a low score. Using minimax and working from the bottom of the game tree up, patching Web first holds the attacker to 1. Patching Mail lets them get 2, and patching DB or HR lets them get 3. So the best move is patching Web. Alpha-beta pruning gets the same answer faster. Once the Web branch gives a 1, the defender knows it can get at least that. In the DB and HR branches, the first attacker move already scores 2 or more, so the rest of those branches get skipped. What surprised me is that the obvious move is to protect DB since it's worth the most, but that's actually worse. Patching Web blocks the only way to DB and leaves the defender a free move to shut down the other side. In security, that's called a choke point. My game only works because the network is tiny, so I could search all the way to the end of the game and get an exact score. A real company has hundreds of servers, so the tree would be way too big to search that far. In that case, you'd have to stop early and use a heuristic evaluation function to guess how good a position is. For this game, a simple one could be adding up the value of every server the attacker can still reach. It wouldn't be perfect, but it would be fast enough to actually use. Regular search works when nothing is fighting back, like mapping out paths in BloodHound. Adversarial search is closer to how security actually works since the attacker is always reacting to what you do. Both showed me the best answer isn't always the obvious one, which is why it helps to have the computer check every option.
